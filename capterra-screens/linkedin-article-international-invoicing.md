How to Invoice International Clients as a Freelancer: Currency, VAT and Getting Paid on Time

---

## Article (paste in the body)

Working with clients in other countries is one of the best things that can happen to a freelancer. Better rates, more interesting projects, and you are no longer limited to the clients in your own city.

But the first international invoice is often where things go wrong. The client's finance team sends it back. The amount that lands in your bank is lower than you expected. Or you are not sure whether you should have charged VAT at all.

I build invoicing software for freelancers and small businesses who work across borders, and these are the questions I see most. Here is a practical checklist, in the order you need it.

### 1. Agree on the currency before you start

The currency is not something to decide when you write the invoice. Put it in your quote or contract.

- **Invoicing in your client's currency** (for example USD for a US client) is easier for them to approve and pay. You carry the exchange-rate risk: the amount that reaches your account in euros can be lower than planned.
- **Invoicing in your own currency** moves that risk to the client.

Whichever you choose, show the currency on every amount, not only on the total, and keep it the same on reminders and credit notes. A surprising number of disputes start with "we thought this was in dollars".

### 2. Check the VAT before you hit send

This is where most freelancers in Europe get nervous, and with reason. The general rules for business-to-business services from an EU country are:

- **Business client in another EU country, with a valid VAT ID:** the VAT is usually reverse-charged. You charge 0% VAT, write "VAT reverse-charged" on the invoice, and add both your VAT ID and your client's. Check their VAT ID first in the EU's VIES database.
- **Business client outside the EU:** services are usually not subject to VAT in your country. You charge no VAT and say so on the invoice.
- **Private individuals:** different rules apply, and you often charge your own country's VAT.

In the Netherlands, reverse-charged sales also go into box 3b of your VAT return and into the ICP declaration. I wrote a full guide on this, with an example of the text to put on your invoice: [Reverse charge VAT (btw verlegd) explained](https://faturapro.app/blog/reverse-charge-vat-invoice-btw-verlegd).

VAT has many exceptions (events, property, digital services to consumers), so check your own case with your tax authority or an accountant.

### 3. Put the right details on the invoice

An international invoice needs everything a local invoice needs, and clients abroad are stricter, not more relaxed, because their finance team does not know you:

- A unique, sequential invoice number and the invoice date
- Your name, address, VAT ID and company registration number
- Your client's legal name and address (and their VAT ID for reverse charge)
- A clear description of the work and the date or period
- Amount excluding VAT, VAT rate and amount, and the total
- Payment term and bank details: IBAN, BIC/SWIFT and the account name

Missing details are one of the most common reasons an invoice gets stuck in approval for weeks.

### 4. Write it in a language your client reads

There is usually no rule that your invoice must be in your own language. Use a language your client's finance team reads. English works for most international clients, but a French or Spanish client will approve an invoice in French or Spanish faster. Some large companies and public bodies have their own requirements, so ask.

### 5. Make it easy to pay

- Within the euro area, a SEPA transfer is cheap and fast.
- Payments from outside often pass through intermediary banks that take a fee. Agree up front who pays those costs.
- Set a clear due date (14 or 30 days) and follow up the day after it passes.

If a business client in the Netherlands pays late, Dutch rules allow a standard €40 collection fee plus statutory commercial interest, as long as you tell the client. Start friendly though: a clear, polite reminder solves most cases. I published [free payment reminder templates in four languages](https://faturapro.app/late-payment-scripts).

### 6. Keep your own books in your own currency

Your bookkeeping and VAT return are in your home currency. Record each foreign-currency invoice at a consistent exchange rate (for example the rate on the invoice date) and note what actually arrived. The difference is an exchange-rate gain or loss. Your accountant will tell you which method to use.

### The short version

Before you send an international invoice, check:

1. The currency is agreed and shown on every amount
2. The VAT treatment is right (reverse charge, outside the EU, or local VAT)
3. All required details are on it, including both VAT IDs when needed
4. It is in a language your client reads
5. Payment details work across borders, and the due date is clear
